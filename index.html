Light Aim

Stop dragging lights around.

Aim them.

Move your cursor over the scene and the light follows it — keep the distance, brightness, finding its way around anything in the way. Press Ctrl+Shift+L, aim, click. That's the whole workflow.

Blender 3.6+ · Every light type · Eevee & Cycles

You always know where the light should land. Getting it there is the annoying part: tweak position, rotation, orbit, check camera view, flip back, do it again.

Light Aim turns that around. You point at the result you want — this surface lit, or highlight here, or shadow falling across this point — and it solves for the light position that produces it, live, while you move.

01 — Placement Modes

Three ways to say
“put the light here”

Same gesture every time — hover the scene, the light tracks your cursor.

Normal

The light sits on the surface normal under your cursor, at your chosen distance — straight-on, even, predictable. The classic “light this thing” move. A Surface Smoothing slider decides how much detail the normal reads.

Reflect

Point at where you want the highlight. The light is placed so your cursor becomes a specular hit for the current camera — the mirror angle is solved for you. This is how you put a glint exactly on the edge of a bottle, a cheekbone, a car panel, instead of hunting for it.

Beta

Shadow

Point at where you want the shadow. Pick a caster — one object, or a whole collection treated as a single shape — and Light Aim searches for the light position that drops its shadow edge right onto your cursor. Nothing else on the market aims a light this way. In beta — it works in both engines, but very complex geometry can still need a nudge.

02 — Shadow-Driven Placement
Beta

Aim the shadow,
not the light.

Shadows are usually the part you fight hardest for and control least. Shadowed mode inverts it: you drive the shadow directly with the mouse and the light goes wherever it has to go to make that happen. It's the newest and most innovative part in the Light Aim, but it ships as beta: solid on ordinary scenes, occasionally in need of a nudge on very complex geometry. Works in both Eevee and Cycles.

To be precise about what the cursor holds: it is the midpoint of the caster's shadow edge. Light Aim takes the shadow boundary the caster throws, finds the middle of it, and solves for the light position that keeps exactly that point under your mouse while you move. So you are placing the edge of the shadow — the rest of the shape follows from the caster's geometry and the size of the light.

03 — Constant Energy

Move the light.
Keep the exposure.

Pull a light back and the scene goes dark; push it in and it blows out. So you fix the position, then fix the power, then the position changed the falloff again. Turn on Constant Energy (E) and that loop disappears.

E Constant Energy — flip it on or off in the middle of a move. The very next frame already accounts for it.
Measured, not guessed

The moment you start moving, probe rays go out through the light’s own frustum and the scene is re-measured every frame. Power is rescaled so the trimmed-mean brightness of the surfaces actually being hit stays at the value it started from.

It matches the render

The measurement follows what the renderer really does — cone falloff and inverse-square distance for spots, incidence angle for area lights. What you are looking at while you aim is what you get.

Distance stays yours

Nothing repositions itself behind your back. The distance you set is the distance you keep — the only thing moving is power.

Common questions

FAQ

Can't find the answer to your question? Feel free to reach out to me on X (Twitter) or Discord.

What exactly does Light Painter paint on?

It paints on the light's gobo texture — the image that controls what a Spot or Area light projects. You're not painting on scene objects or meshes. The light itself is your canvas, so there are no limitations from object materials or emission shaders. Everything happens on the light.

Is this the same "Light Painter" add-on from the Blender Extensions platform?

No — those are two completely separate add-ons by different developers. The one on Blender Extensions is a light placement helper that puts lights where you click on a surface. This Light Painter does something fundamentally different: it masks and shapes the light itself, giving you full control over what areas get lit and which don't. Same name, very different tools.

Can I paint from any viewport angle, or only camera view?

You can paint from any angle — just use your mouse cursor in the viewport. There are two projection modes: Camera Space (what you see on screen maps 1:1 to the paint result) and Light Space (paint maps relative to the light's own projection, consistent regardless of viewing angle). Pick whichever fits your needs.

How do I set it up? Do I place lights first?

Yes — you place your Spot or Area lights in the scene as usual, then select a light and start painting inside its projection. There's no special setup, no node wiring, no material graph. Install the add-on, pick a light, add a layer, paint.

Can I paint with color? How do multicolored lights work?

Pick any color and paint — you can do it all on one layer or spread colors across separate layers. Each layer has its own blend mode, so you control exactly how colors mix. Under the hood, each light gets its own stack of gobo textures that you paint on independently.

Are there limitations on shadow softness or light control?

No — since you're painting the gobo texture, everything that Blender's Cycles engine can do with that texture still applies. Light size, shadow softness, intensity, and falloff all work normally. Light Painter just gives you a much faster, more visual way to create and edit the gobo itself.

Can I export painted lights to Unity or Unreal?

Light Painter uses a layer stack built on top of Blender's light shader nodes, so the multi-layer setup itself won't transfer to another engine. The recommended workflow: merge your layers into a single image (Full version has Merge Visible), then export that texture and assign it as a light cookie in your engine. Both Unity ("Cookies") and Unreal ("Light Functions" / gobo textures) natively support this — just drop the exported image onto a light and you're set.

Does it work with multiple lights in the scene?

Yes — any number of lights. Each light can keep its own layer stack, so you can jump between lights, add or edit layers on each one, and paint each independently. There's no setup per light: select it, add a layer, start painting.

Do I need to set up geometry nodes or shaders?

No. Light Painter doesn't use geometry nodes. You install the add-on, pick a light, and start painting.

Which Blender versions and render engines are supported?

Blender 3.6.x on Windows, macOS and Linux. The painting side runs on Cycles and applies to Spot and Area lights — those are the lights that project a gobo texture. Light Aim, the placement side, is engine-independent: it works in both Eevee and Cycles, with Point, Sun, Spot and Area lights.

Can I use Light Painter commercially?

Yes — one license covers personal and commercial use, including client work, studios, and freelance projects.

How do updates work?

A one-time purchase gives you lifetime updates — every future update is free. You'll get access through your Blender Market account.

Will it slow down my viewport?

Painting runs in real time and is designed to stay responsive. Layer compositing is handled efficiently per-light, and you can work on complex scenes with multiple painted lights without significant overhead.

Which lights does it work with?

All four of them — Point, Sun, Spot and Area — in Blender 3.6 and newer. Aiming only needs a light and some geometry to hit, so it isn't tied to a light type or a render engine. The extras that only some lights have (cone angle and blend on spots, size on area lights) simply appear when the selected light has them.

Does it change my light's settings permanently?

Only what you confirm. While the modal runs it drives position, rotation and — if Constant Energy is on — power. Escape or right-click restores everything exactly as it was before you pressed Ctrl+Shift+L. Left-click or Enter commits.

Will it work in my scene if things keep getting in the way?

That case is handled directly. Aiming rays skip the object under your cursor and, in Shadowed mode, the shadow caster; you can also nominate an object or collection to ignore entirely, so glass, ceilings and proxy geometry stop intercepting rays. If something still blocks the light's path to the target, the placement is redirected or pulled in and the viewport header tells you it happened.

Does it work with Cycles and EEVEE?

Aiming is a placement tool — it moves the light object and reads scene geometry, so it's not tied to a render engine, and it works while you're rendering in either. Constant Energy measures brightness using the same maths the renderer uses for spot and area lights, so its readings match what you get out of Cycles.

Is Light Aim a separate add-on?

No — it's a new feature of Light Painter, living in its own Light Aim sidebar tab. Same install, same licence, no extra cost. Painting the light and placing it are two halves of the same workflow, so they belong in the same tool.

How do I actually aim a light?

Select a Spot or Area light and press Ctrl+Shift+L — the default shortcut, which you can rebind in the add-on preferences — or hit the button in the sidebar. The light then follows your cursor across the scene. 1 / 2 / 3 switch between Reflect, Normal and Shadowed; the scroll wheel drives power, Alt+wheel the distance, Shift+wheel the cone angle or area size, Ctrl+wheel the spot blend. Left-click or Enter commits; Esc or right-click puts everything back.

What's the difference between Normal and Reflect mode?

Normal puts the light straight out along the surface normal under your cursor, at whatever distance you've set — it hits that spot square-on, which is the predictable "just light this" answer. Reflect instead solves the mirror angle for the current camera, so the point you're aiming at becomes a specular highlight. Same gesture, two different questions: light this, versus put a glint exactly here.

Does Constant Energy really match what the render shows?

It measures brightness with the same falloff maths the renderer uses — inverse-square with the spot cone term for spots, N·L across the emitter for area lights — sampled over the light's frustum and averaged with the outliers trimmed. Push the light closer or further and the power compensates, so the lit area keeps roughly the exposure you had. It's an estimate rather than a render, but close enough that you stop re-tuning power after every move.

The light jitters when I move over detailed geometry — can I damp that?

That's the surface normal changing under your cursor on noisy meshes. The Surface Smoothing slider averages normals over a wider area: 0 follows every bump, higher values read the broader shape of the surface. Turn it up on foliage, fabric, displaced terrain and sculpted detail.

Can I still move the light by hand afterwards?

Yes. Aiming only sets the light object's transform — and its power, if Constant Energy is on. No constraints, drivers or hidden helpers are left behind. Grab it with G, keyframe it, parent it to a rig, do whatever you normally would, and aim again whenever you want to re-point it.

Why Shadow Mode is marked beta?

Because it's brand new and, under the hood, the most complex thing here — it's solving an inverse problem every frame: you say where the result should land, and it works backwards to a light position that produces it. It's been tested hard and it holds up in normal scenes, but with complex geometry, occluders or an unusual setup it can still land somewhere you didn't mean. Beta is me being honest about that rather than pretending the edge cases don't exist. It works today, it will keep getting better with each update, and everything it changes is undoable with Esc.
